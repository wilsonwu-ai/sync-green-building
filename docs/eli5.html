The building with a pulse

You put a finger on your phone camera. A 21‑storey tower starts beating your heart.

How it works, in three steps

STEP 1

Your finger is a sensor

Phone torch on, fingertip over the lens. Every heartbeat pushes blood through your finger and slightly dims the red light reaching the camera. Count the dips, you have a pulse. No hardware.

STEP 2

The tower wears it

153 windows, 9 across and 17 up. The beat starts at the middle floors and travels out toward the roof and the street, like a real pulse moving through a body.

STEP 3

Then it takes over

The building slows its breathing to six breaths a minute and stops following you. Breathe with a slow rhythm and your heart rate drops. That is the whole trick, and it is measurable.

Drag the slider. That's the piece.

Why it looks like this

153lit windows
9×17bays × floors
30frames per second
~500mviewing distance

153 pixels is not a screen. At that size, seen from across the Charles at night, text is unreadable, faces are unreadable, video is unreadable. What survives the distance is colour, vertical movement, and rhythm.

Which happens to be exactly the vocabulary a body has. So the grid is treated as an anatomy, not a display: nine wide and seventeen tall is a torso. Head at the crown, heart at floor 12, feet at the base.

The honest part. Heart rate from a phone camera is reliable. Heart rate variability from a phone camera is not, so the calm score is a rough proxy and is never presented as a medical number.
